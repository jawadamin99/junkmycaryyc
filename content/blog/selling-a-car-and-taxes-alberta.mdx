Most people selling a car in Alberta will owe no tax on it whatsoever, and won’t need to report anything.

That’s the honest headline, and it’s worth stating plainly before the detail, because the anxiety around this question is disproportionate to the actual risk. But “most people” isn’t everyone, and there are a handful of situations where the answer changes completely. Those are the ones worth understanding.

This is general information, not tax advice. Tax rules change and individual circumstances vary enormously. For anything involving a business vehicle, an appreciated vehicle, or a substantial sum, speak to an accountant or check directly with the Canada Revenue Agency.

## First, the Alberta Advantage Nobody Mentions

Alberta has no provincial sales tax, and that has a genuine effect on private vehicle transactions here.

When two private individuals buy and sell a used car in Alberta, there is typically no sales tax at all on the transaction. No PST, because Alberta doesn’t have one. And GST generally doesn’t apply to a private sale between individuals who aren’t GST registrants.

This is not the case elsewhere in Canada. In several provinces, buying a used car privately triggers a provincial tax bill at registration that can run into thousands of dollars on a mid-priced vehicle. Alberta buyers simply don’t face that.

Buying from a dealership is different — that’s a commercial sale, and GST applies.

## Do You Owe Tax on the Money You Received?

For the overwhelming majority of sellers: no.

Your car is what tax law calls personal-use property. When you sell personal-use property, tax only becomes relevant if you sold it for more than you paid for it — and vehicles almost never appreciate. A car you bought for $28,000 and sold for $6,000 has produced a $22,000 loss in ordinary terms, and there’s no gain to tax.

You generally don’t report the sale. You don’t declare the money as income. The cash from selling your old vehicle isn’t earnings — it’s the conversion of something you already owned into a different form.

There’s also a rule in the personal-use property provisions that treats both the cost and the proceeds as being at least $1,000 for these purposes, which has the practical effect of eliminating tax consequences on most small transactions entirely.

## The Loss You Cannot Claim

Here’s the flip side, and it’s the point that surprises people most:

You cannot deduct the loss on a personal vehicle.

That depreciation is real money that genuinely left your life. It is not a capital loss you can apply against gains elsewhere, and it isn’t deductible in any form. Losses on personal-use property are specifically excluded.

People occasionally hear that vehicles depreciate and assume there must be some tax relief attached. For a personal car, there isn’t. The asymmetry is deliberate: gains on personal-use property can be taxable, losses on it never are.

## When You Actually Do Owe Tax: Appreciated Vehicles

Rare, but real, and increasingly worth knowing about.

If you sell a vehicle for more than you paid, the difference can be a capital gain, and a portion of a capital gain is taxable income. Situations where this genuinely happens:

- Classic and collector cars. A restored vehicle bought two decades ago and sold today may well have appreciated substantially.
- Rare or limited-production models that hold or gain value.
- Unusual market conditions. During the used-vehicle supply crunch of recent years, some people genuinely sold ordinary cars for close to — or above — what they paid.
- A vehicle inherited or received as a gift, where your cost base is determined differently and may be lower than you assume.

If you’re in this territory, the gain is calculated from your adjusted cost base — generally what you paid, plus certain qualifying costs — against your proceeds. Restoration and improvement costs may factor in, which is precisely why receipts matter on a project car.

The inclusion rate for capital gains has been subject to proposed changes in recent years, so confirm the current rate rather than relying on what you remember. And if the sum is meaningful, involve an accountant before you file.

## Business Vehicles Are a Completely Different World

If the vehicle was used in a business or claimed against self-employment income, everything above stops applying and a much more involved set of rules takes over.

Capital cost allowance and recapture matter here. If you claimed CCA — tax depreciation — on the vehicle, selling it can trigger recapture. In simple terms: if you deducted more depreciation than the vehicle actually lost in value, the excess gets added back to your income in the year of sale. That can produce a genuine tax bill from a sale you assumed was neutral.

GST obligations can also apply. If you’re a GST registrant and the vehicle was used in commercial activity, you may be required to collect and remit GST on the sale.

Mixed personal and business use requires apportionment, which is exactly the kind of calculation worth paying someone to get right.

If any part of this describes your situation, talk to your accountant before completing the sale rather than after. The tax outcome can influence how and when you sell.

## Trade-In vs. Private Sale: A Tax Angle Worth Knowing

When you buy from a dealership in Alberta and trade a vehicle in, the GST is generally calculated on the net amount after your trade-in allowance, not on the full price of the new vehicle.

That has a real consequence. If a dealer offers you less on trade than you could get privately, the gap isn’t necessarily as large as it looks, because trading in reduces the GST you pay on the purchase.

The comparison worth running is: private sale price versus trade-in allowance plus the GST you avoid. Sometimes the private sale still wins comfortably. Sometimes it doesn’t. Do the arithmetic before assuming.

This only applies to a purchase from a registered dealer. It’s irrelevant if you’re not buying anything.

## Scrapping or Junking a Car

If you’re selling an end-of-life vehicle to a [scrap or salvage buyer](/services/cash-for-scrap-cars-calgary), the tax analysis is short: you’re almost certainly disposing of personal-use property at a substantial loss, there’s no gain, the loss isn’t deductible, and there’s nothing to report.

Keep the bill of sale regardless. It matters for reasons that have nothing to do with tax — it’s your documented proof of when the vehicle left your ownership, which protects you if that VIN ever resurfaces.

The exception, again, is a vehicle that was on your business books. Then CCA and GST considerations may still apply even on a scrap sale.

## Donating a Vehicle Instead

If you donate a vehicle to a registered charity, you can receive a donation receipt — and it’s worth understanding what that’s actually worth.

The receipt reflects what the charity nets, which on an end-of-life vehicle is often modest. And a donation receipt generates a non-refundable tax credit, not a dollar-for-dollar refund. You receive a percentage back, not the receipted amount.

On a genuinely worthless vehicle, donation may beat a low cash offer. On most, it doesn’t. Run both numbers rather than assuming the charitable route is automatically better.

## What to Keep

Simple, and useful for years:

- Your bill of sale, showing the date, VIN, odometer, price and both parties
- Your original purchase documentation, particularly on anything that might appreciate
- Receipts for improvements on a restored or collector vehicle
- Business vehicle records — CCA history, business-use percentage, GST treatment

Store them somewhere you’ll find them, not in the glovebox of a car you no longer own.

## The Short Version

Sold your ordinary daily driver at a loss, as a private individual? No tax, nothing to report, and no loss to claim.

Sold something that appreciated, or something that was on your business books? Different conversation entirely — and one worth having with an accountant before you sign anything.
